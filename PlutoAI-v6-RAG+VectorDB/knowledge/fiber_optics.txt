                          A Two-Page Primer

1. WHAT AN OPTICAL FIBRE IS

An optical fibre is a thin strand of ultra-pure glass that carries information
as pulses of light. It has two concentric regions: a core of refractive index
n1, and a cladding of slightly lower index n2. Light entering the core within
a certain acceptance angle strikes the core-cladding boundary and is totally
internally reflected, so it stays trapped in the core and follows the fibre
even around bends.

Standard telecom fibre has a core of about 9 micrometres and a cladding of
125 micrometres - roughly the thickness of a human hair. A protective acrylate
coating brings it to 250 micrometres. Everything beyond that (tubes, strength
members, jacketing) is packaging that keeps the glass in a benign mechanical
environment for thirty years or more.

Two families exist:

  Single-mode fibre (SMF). ~9 um core. Only one light path propagates, so
  pulses stay sharp over long distances. All carrier outside-plant is SMF.

  Multimode fibre (MMF). 50 or 62.5 um core. Many light paths propagate at
  slightly different speeds, blurring pulses. Cheap transceivers, but reach is
  limited to hundreds of metres. Used inside buildings and data centres.


2. WHY IT BEAT COPPER

Bandwidth. Light frequencies are enormous, so a single fibre can carry many
terabits per second. A copper pair cannot approach this.

Loss. Good fibre attenuates about 0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm. Signals travel 80-100
km between amplifiers. Copper loss rises steeply with both frequency and
distance, which is why DSL speed collapses over a few hundred metres.

Immunity. Glass carries no current, so fibre is unaffected by electromagnetic
interference, ground loops and lightning, and it emits nothing that can be
picked up.

Size and weight. One small cable replaces a large copper bundle - the original
reason city carriers adopted fibre in the 1970s, when duct space, not
bandwidth, was the binding constraint.

Operating cost. Passive plant needs no power in the field and fails far less
often than copper, which dominates the long-run economics.


3. A SHORT HISTORY

  1841-1854  Colladon, and later Tyndall, demonstrate light guided along a
             falling water jet - total internal reflection in public view.
  1954       Van Heel, Hopkins and Kapany add cladding to glass fibres, making
             practical fibre bundles possible.
  1966       Charles Kao argues that fibre loss is caused by impurities, not
             by glass itself, and sets a 20 dB/km target. Nobel Prize, 2009.
  1970       Corning produces fibre at 17 dB/km. The same year, semiconductor
             lasers run continuously at room temperature. Source and medium
             arrive together.
  1977       First live telephone traffic over fibre, in California and the UK.
  1987       The erbium-doped fibre amplifier is demonstrated, allowing optical
             signals to be amplified without conversion to electrical form.
  1990s      Wavelength division multiplexing: dozens of colours on one fibre,
             all amplified by one EDFA. Cost per bit collapses.
  2001       Speculative long-haul overbuild collapses, leaving large amounts
             of unlit "dark fibre" in the ground.
  2008 on    Coherent detection and digital signal processing push a single
             wavelength from 10 Gbit/s to 100G, 400G, 800G and now 1.6 Tbit/s.
  2000s on   Fibre reaches the home at scale, first in Japan and Korea, then
             progressively across Europe and North America.


4. THE PHYSICS THAT LIMITS A LINK

Attenuation. Dominated by Rayleigh scattering off microscopic density
variations frozen into the glass. It falls as wavelength rises, which is why
telecom uses 1310 nm and 1550 nm rather than visible light. Splices,
connectors and splitters add discrete loss on top.

Chromatic dispersion. Different wavelengths travel at slightly different
speeds, so a pulse spreads in time and eventually overlaps its neighbour.
Standard fibre has near-zero chromatic dispersion at 1310 nm and about
17 ps/(nm.km) at 1550 nm.

Polarisation-mode dispersion. Small asymmetries in the core make the two
polarisation states travel at different speeds. It varies with temperature and
vibration, so it must be tracked dynamically rather than compensated once.

Nonlinearity. Above a certain launch power, the refractive index of glass
becomes intensity-dependent and channels begin to distort each other. This
caps how much power can be launched, and therefore caps reach and capacity.

Every design reduces to one inequality:

    Transmit power - total loss - penalties  >=  receiver sensitivity + margin


5. THE MAIN BUILDING BLOCKS

  Transmitter    A laser diode, usually a distributed-feedback (DFB) type,
                 modulated directly or by an external modulator.
  Receiver       A PIN or avalanche photodiode converting light back to
                 current. Coherent receivers instead mix the signal with a
                 local laser to recover phase as well as amplitude.
  Amplifier      EDFAs amplify a whole band of wavelengths optically, without
                 decoding the signal. Raman amplifiers pump the transmission
                 fibre itself.
  Multiplexer    Combines many wavelengths onto one fibre (WDM). Dense WDM
                 fits 80 or more channels into the C band alone.
  ROADM          A switch that routes individual wavelengths between fibres
                 under software control, without electrical conversion.
  Splitter       A passive device that divides one fibre among many - the
                 heart of a passive optical network. Needs no power.
  Transceiver    A pluggable module (SFP, QSFP-DD, OSFP) combining transmitter
                 and receiver in a standard form factor.


6. HOW THE ACCESS NETWORK USES IT

Most homes are connected by a passive optical network (PON), a tree topology:

    Optical line terminal (OLT) in the exchange
        -> feeder fibre
            -> passive splitter (typically 1:32 or 1:64)
                -> distribution fibre and drop
                    -> optical network terminal (ONT) in the home

Downstream, the OLT broadcasts to every ONT, and each device keeps only the
frames addressed to it, with encryption providing privacy. Upstream, ONTs share
the fibre by time-division multiple access: the OLT grants each one a
transmission window, having first measured its distance so that bursts from
near and far subscribers arrive without colliding.

The economics work because the splitter is passive. One fibre and one OLT port
are shared across dozens of homes, with no powered equipment between the
exchange and the customer.

Generations in service: GPON (2.5G down / 1.25G up), XGS-PON (10G symmetric),
and now 50G PON. Each uses different wavelengths, so they coexist on the same
glass and subscribers can be migrated one at a time without touching the
outside plant. That property is the single most important reason fibre
infrastructure holds its value: the electronics will be replaced several times
over the life of the cable.
