1. SCOPE AND HOW TO READ THIS DOCUMENT
================================================================================

This document is written for engineers who design, build, operate or make
technology decisions about optical networks in a telecom service provider
context. It assumes familiarity with digital communications and IP networking
but does not assume a background in photonics.

The weighting is deliberate. Roughly half the technical content concerns the
carrier access and aggregation domain - passive optical networks, fibre-to-the-
x topologies, optical distribution network engineering, and the hardware and
software that terminate and manage them. Long-haul, submarine and data centre
interconnect are covered because access networks do not exist in isolation and
because the same component technologies increasingly cross domains, but they
are treated as context rather than as the main subject.

Numbers quoted for component performance, loss budgets and costs are
representative industry figures intended for order-of-magnitude reasoning and
architectural comparison. They are not a substitute for vendor datasheets,
the applicable ITU-T or IEEE recommendation, or a link budget computed against
the actual plant.

A note on terminology: this document uses "fibre" in prose and "fiber" where it
appears inside a standardised term or product name. ITU-T recommendations are
cited by number; the recommendation text itself is authoritative.

2. HISTORY AND INDUSTRIAL EVOLUTION
================================================================================

2.1 Guided Light Before Glass Fibre

The physical principle behind optical fibre - total internal reflection - was
demonstrated publicly in 1841 by Daniel Colladon in Geneva, who showed that
light injected into a jet of water falling from a tank would follow the curve
of the jet rather than travelling straight. Jacques Babinet made related
observations, and John Tyndall repeated the demonstration at the Royal
Institution in 1854, which is why the effect is often attributed to him in
English-language sources.

Nineteenth-century engineers understood the principle but had neither a
coherent light source nor a low-loss medium. Alexander Graham Bell's
photophone of 1880 transmitted speech on a beam of sunlight modulated by a
vibrating mirror, achieving a few hundred metres in clear air. Bell considered
it his most important invention. It was also completely impractical: the
atmosphere is a hostile transmission medium, and there was no way to store,
switch or amplify the signal.

The first practical uses of guided light were short-range and non-
communications. In the 1920s Clarence Hansell and John Logie Baird
independently proposed bundles of transparent rods for image transmission. In
1954 Abraham van Heel in Delft and Harold Hopkins and Narinder Singh Kapany at
Imperial College London published the decisive refinement: cladding the glass
core with a lower-index material. Uncladded rods leaked light wherever they
touched each other or a support; cladding confined the light and made bundles
usable. The medical fibrescope followed, and with it the first commercial
market for optical fibre.

2.2 The 20 dB/km Threshold

Losses in early fibre were of the order of 1000 dB/km. At that attenuation a
signal is extinguished within metres. The medium was regarded as fundamentally
unsuited to telecommunications.

In July 1966 Charles Kao and George Hockham, working at Standard
Telecommunication Laboratories in Harlow, England, published a paper arguing
that the loss was not intrinsic to glass but was dominated by impurities -
principally transition-metal ions such as iron, copper and chromium - and by
manufacturing imperfections. They set a target: if attenuation could be brought
below 20 dB/km, optical fibre would outperform coaxial cable for long-distance
transmission. Kao then spent years travelling to glass manufacturers making the
case for ultra-pure silica. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2009.

The target was met in 1970 by Robert Maurer, Donald Keck and Peter Schultz at
Corning Glass Works, who produced a titanium-doped silica fibre at 17 dB/km.
Within a few years germanium doping and improved vapour deposition processes
brought attenuation to around 4 dB/km, and by the end of the decade to under
1 dB/km. The same year, 1970, Zhores Alferov's group and Izuo Hayashi and
Morton Panish at Bell Labs demonstrated semiconductor lasers capable of
continuous-wave operation at room temperature. A practical source and a
practical medium arrived simultaneously.

2.3 Generations of Transmission Systems

First generation (late 1970s). Multimode fibre at 850 nm, GaAlAs lasers or
LEDs, around 45 Mbit/s, repeater spacing of roughly 10 km. General Telephone
and Electronics carried live traffic in Long Beach, California in 1977; the UK
Post Office ran a link near Martlesham the same year. The commercial case was
made not by bandwidth but by duct congestion in cities, where fibre relieved
physical space pressure that copper could not.

Second generation (early 1980s). A shift to 1310 nm, where silica has a loss
minimum around 0.5 dB/km and, critically, near-zero chromatic dispersion in
standard fibre. Single-mode fibre displaced multimode for telecom use.
Bit rates reached 1.7 Gbit/s with repeater spacing of 50 km.

Third generation (mid to late 1980s). A shift to 1550 nm, the absolute loss
minimum of silica at about 0.2 dB/km. This created a dispersion problem,
because standard fibre has substantial chromatic dispersion at 1550 nm, which
drove the development of dispersion-shifted fibre and of narrow-linewidth
distributed-feedback lasers. Systems reached 2.5 Gbit/s over 100 km without
repeaters.

Fourth generation (1990s). Two innovations converged. The erbium-doped fibre
amplifier, demonstrated at Southampton and Bell Labs in 1987, made it possible
to amplify optical signals directly without converting to electrical form. And
because the EDFA has gain across a band roughly 30 nm wide, it made wavelength
division multiplexing economically compelling: one amplifier could carry dozens
of channels. The cost-per-bit curve broke. Commercial WDM systems appeared in
1995-96 with 8 and then 16 channels; by 2000, 80- and 160-channel dense WDM
systems were shipping. The first transatlantic fibre cable, TAT-8, had entered
service in 1988 with a capacity of 280 Mbit/s; within fifteen years single
cables carried thousands of times that.

The build-out became a speculative bubble. Between 1998 and 2001 carriers and
new entrants laid enormous quantities of long-haul fibre on the assumption that
internet traffic was doubling every three months. It was not. The collapse in
2001 left large volumes of unlit "dark fibre" in the ground, bankrupted several
carriers, and depressed optical component pricing for most of a decade. The
overbuild had one lasting benefit: cheap long-haul capacity underwrote the
first generation of large-scale content distribution and cloud services.

Fifth generation (2008 onwards). Coherent detection returned. Direct-detection
receivers discard optical phase; coherent receivers mix the incoming signal
with a local oscillator laser and recover amplitude, phase and both
polarisations. Combined with high-speed analogue-to-digital conversion and
digital signal processing, this allowed chromatic dispersion and polarisation-
mode dispersion to be compensated electronically rather than optically, and
allowed higher-order modulation formats. 100 Gbit/s per wavelength using
dual-polarisation QPSK became the long-haul standard from around 2010.
Subsequent generations moved to 16QAM, 64QAM, probabilistic constellation
shaping and baud rates above 100 Gbaud, reaching 800 Gbit/s and then
1.6 Tbit/s per wavelength on commercial hardware.

2.4 The Access Network Story

Long-haul fibre economics worked from the start because cost is shared across
enormous traffic volumes. The access network is the opposite: cost is borne per
subscriber, and the subscriber's willingness to pay is bounded.

Fibre-to-the-home therefore arrived late and unevenly. Japan led, with NTT
launching commercial FTTH in 2001 and passing ten million subscribers by 2008,
supported by government policy and by high urban density. South Korea followed
a similar path. In the United States, Verizon's FiOS programme from 2004 was a
large single-operator bet that competitors did not match; most US incumbents
chose fibre-to-the-node with VDSL over the remaining copper. Europe fragmented:
Sweden, Lithuania and Spain moved early, Germany and the UK much later,
with the UK's large-scale full-fibre build only accelerating after 2018 under
regulatory and altnet competitive pressure.

The technology choice in access settled on the passive optical network. The
alternative, point-to-point active Ethernet, gives each subscriber a dedicated
fibre and a dedicated port. It is simpler and offers more capacity per user,
and it dominates in parts of Scandinavia and in certain municipal builds, but
it consumes far more fibre in the feeder and far more headend port capacity.
PON shares one feeder fibre and one line-terminal port among typically 32 to 64
subscribers using passive optical splitters that need no power, no cooling and
no maintenance in the field. That economic argument has proved decisive at
scale.

PON itself evolved through a sequence of standards. APON and BPON (ITU-T G.983)
in the late 1990s carried ATM cells at 155-622 Mbit/s and saw limited
deployment. EPON (IEEE 802.3ah, 2004) carried native Ethernet at 1 Gbit/s
symmetric and dominated in China, Japan and Korea. GPON (ITU-T G.984, 2003-04)
offered 2.488 Gbit/s downstream and 1.244 Gbit/s upstream with an efficient
framing layer, and became the global volume standard. XG-PON and then XGS-PON
(ITU-T G.9807, 2016) took this to 10 Gbit/s, symmetric in the XGS variant, on
new wavelengths that coexist with GPON on the same fibre plant. NG-PON2 (ITU-T
G.989) specified tunable multi-wavelength TWDM operation; it found niche use in
mobile transport but its optics cost prevented mass adoption. The industry
then converged on 50G PON (ITU-T G.9804 series, first recommendations consented
in April 2021) as the next mass-market step, with the G.9804.3 physical media
dependent specification continuing to be amended through 2025-26 to add
upstream options and coexistence wavelengths.

2.5 Where the Industry Stands in 2026

Several trends define the current period.

Access is mid-migration. GPON remains the largest installed base but is
capacity-constrained in dense markets; XGS-PON is the volume deployment choice
for new builds and overlays; 50G PON has moved from trials into first
commercial deployments, initially for business and high-value segments, with
symmetric variants expected to follow.

Coherent optics have become pluggable. What was a dedicated transponder shelf
is now a module in a router faceplate. 400ZR and OpenZR+ made this ordinary;
800ZR/ZR+ is shipping in volume and extending pluggable coherent from metro
into regional and some long-haul applications, and OIF has published the
1600ZR implementation agreement for 1.6 Tbit/s per wavelength.

The demand mix has shifted. For two decades access bandwidth growth was driven
by video streaming. The current marginal driver in transport and interconnect
is AI infrastructure - training clusters, inference serving and the data centre
interconnect between them - which has pulled hyperscalers into direct
investment in fibre routes, hollow-core fibre manufacturing and optical
switching in ways that reshape the supplier landscape carriers operate in.

Fibre assets have been financialised. Infrastructure funds, network carve-outs
and wholesale-only operators have separated the passive plant from the retail
service in many markets, which changes how carriers plan, cost and compete.

3. PHYSICAL FOUNDATIONS
================================================================================

3.1 Guidance

An optical fibre is a cylindrical dielectric waveguide: a core of refractive
index n1 surrounded by a cladding of index n2, with n1 > n2 by a small margin,
typically 0.3 to 1 percent. Light entering within the acceptance cone is
confined by total internal reflection at the core-cladding boundary.

The ray-optics picture is useful but incomplete. A full treatment solves
Maxwell's equations for the cylindrical boundary conditions and yields discrete
guided modes. The governing parameter is the normalised frequency, or V number:

    V = (2 * pi * a / lambda) * sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2)

where a is the core radius and lambda the free-space wavelength. For V < 2.405
only the fundamental LP01 mode propagates and the fibre is single-mode. The
quantity sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2) is the numerical aperture, which sets the acceptance
angle and therefore how efficiently light can be coupled in.

Single-mode fibre for telecom has a core diameter of about 8-10 micrometres and
a cladding diameter of 125 micrometres. Multimode fibre has a 50 or 62.5
micrometre core; it accepts far more light from cheap sources but suffers modal
dispersion, which restricts it to short reach. Carrier outside-plant is
essentially all single-mode. Multimode survives inside buildings, data centres
and some intra-site applications where reach is under a few hundred metres and
transceiver cost dominates.

Mode field diameter matters more than core diameter in practice. It describes
the actual radial extent of the optical field, which extends into the cladding.
Splicing fibres with mismatched MFD produces loss even when the geometry looks
correct - a common cause of unexplained splice loss when different fibre types
meet at a joint.

3.2 Attenuation

Attenuation in dB/km is the primary budget constraint in any link. It comprises:

Rayleigh scattering. Scattering from microscopic density fluctuations frozen
into the glass during manufacture. It scales as 1/lambda^4, which is why longer
wavelengths are preferred, and it is the dominant intrinsic loss mechanism
across the telecom bands. It cannot be engineered away in solid silica; it sets
the floor of about 0.14-0.15 dB/km at 1550 nm for the best conventional fibre.

Infrared absorption. Silica's molecular vibrational absorption rises steeply
beyond about 1600 nm and closes the long-wavelength end of the usable spectrum.

Ultraviolet absorption. Electronic transitions, significant only at short
wavelengths.

OH- absorption. The hydroxyl ion produces a strong absorption peak near
1383 nm. Legacy fibre has a pronounced "water peak" here. Low-water-peak fibre
conforming to ITU-T G.652.D suppresses it, opening the E band for CWDM and for
PON wavelength planning.

Bending loss. Macrobending, where the fibre is coiled tighter than its minimum
bend radius, and microbending, from lateral pressure on the coating. Both
couple guided light into radiation modes. Bend loss rises sharply with
wavelength, so a marginal bend can pass a 1310 nm test and fail at 1625 nm -
which is exactly why 1625 or 1650 nm is used as the out-of-band monitoring
wavelength on live PON plant.

Splice, connector and component loss. Discrete rather than distributed, but
budgeted the same way. Typical values: fusion splice 0.05-0.1 dB, mechanical
splice 0.1-0.3 dB, connector pair 0.2-0.5 dB, 1:32 splitter about 17-18 dB
including excess loss.

3.3 Transmission Windows

    Band   Name              Range (nm)     Typical carrier use
    ----   ----------------  ------------   --------------------------------
    O      Original          1260 - 1360    GPON/XGS-PON upstream, 50G PON,
                                            short-reach grey optics
    E      Extended          1360 - 1460    Requires low-water-peak fibre
    S      Short             1460 - 1530    XGS-PON downstream, Raman pumps
    C      Conventional      1530 - 1565    DWDM, EDFA gain band, coherent
    L      Long              1565 - 1625    C+L band extension, DWDM
    U      Ultra-long        1625 - 1675    Out-of-band monitoring (OTDR)

The C band is the centre of gravity of the transport industry because it
combines minimum loss with EDFA gain. C+L band systems roughly double usable
spectrum at the cost of a second amplifier chain and additional Raman tilt
management, and are now standard on high-capacity routes.

3.4 Dispersion

Dispersion spreads a pulse in time, causing intersymbol interference. Three
forms matter.

Modal dispersion exists only in multimode fibre: different modes travel
different path lengths. Graded-index profiles reduce it substantially by making
higher-order modes travel through lower-index regions at higher group velocity.
It is the reason multimode reach is measured in hundreds of metres.

Chromatic dispersion arises because refractive index varies with wavelength and
because any modulated signal occupies finite spectral width. It is expressed in
ps/(nm.km). Standard G.652 fibre has zero chromatic dispersion near 1310 nm and
about +17 ps/(nm.km) at 1550 nm. Its impact scales with the square of the
symbol rate, so it becomes the binding constraint quickly as rates increase.
Historically it was managed with dispersion-compensating fibre modules, which
added loss and latency. Coherent systems compensate it in the DSP, and modern
lines are typically built without optical dispersion compensation at all.

Polarisation-mode dispersion arises from residual asymmetry in the fibre core
and from external stress. The two orthogonal polarisation states travel at
slightly different velocities. PMD is stochastic, varies with temperature and
mechanical disturbance, and accumulates with the square root of distance. It is
quoted in ps/sqrt(km). Modern fibre achieves under 0.1 ps/sqrt(km); older
installed fibre from the 1980s and early 1990s can be an order of magnitude
worse and is frequently the limiting factor when carriers try to upgrade legacy
routes to high baud rates. Coherent DSP tracks and compensates PMD dynamically,
which is one of the main reasons coherent technology unlocked capacity on
existing plant.

3.5 Nonlinear Effects

Above a certain launch power, silica's refractive index becomes intensity-
dependent and the fibre stops behaving linearly. This sets the upper bound on
optical signal-to-noise ratio and therefore on reach and spectral efficiency.

Self-phase modulation: the pulse modulates its own phase, broadening its
spectrum and interacting with chromatic dispersion.

Cross-phase modulation: one WDM channel's intensity modulates the phase of its
neighbours. It scales with channel count and is worse at tight channel spacing.

Four-wave mixing: three waves generate a fourth at a new frequency. If that
frequency lands on an occupied channel, it appears as interference. FWM is
efficient when channels are equally spaced and dispersion is low - which is why
dispersion-shifted fibre (G.653), designed to zero dispersion at 1550 nm,
turned out to be a poor choice for DWDM and was largely abandoned. A small
non-zero dispersion is beneficial because it destroys the phase matching FWM
requires.

Stimulated Raman scattering: energy transfers from shorter to longer
wavelengths, producing gain tilt across a wide band. It is a problem in C+L
systems and an exploitable resource in Raman amplifiers.

Stimulated Brillouin scattering: interaction with acoustic phonons, generating
a strong backward-propagating wave. It has a low threshold for narrowband
signals and is mitigated by phase dithering the source.

The practical consequence is the "nonlinear Shannon limit": increasing launch
power improves OSNR up to a point, beyond which nonlinear penalties dominate
and performance degrades. Optimising a line means finding that per-channel
power, and it is why simply turning up the amplifiers does not extend reach.

3.6 The Link Budget

Every fibre design reduces to one inequality:

    Transmit power (dBm) - Total loss (dB) - Penalties (dB)
        >= Receiver sensitivity (dBm) + Margin (dB)

Total loss sums fibre attenuation over distance, splice loss, connector loss,
splitter loss and any passive filters. Penalties account for dispersion,
crosstalk and reflections. Margin covers ageing, temperature variation, future
repairs and re-splices - typically 3 dB in access plant, and worth defending
in design reviews because it is the first thing sacrificed when a route is
longer than planned.

For an access engineer the loss budget is expressed as a PON optical power
budget class. Class B+ gives 28 dB, Class C+ gives 32 dB, and Class D and
extended budgets go further. The budget is consumed mostly by the splitter: a
1:32 split costs about 17.5 dB before any fibre is traversed, and a 1:64 split
about 20.5 dB. This is why split ratio, not distance, is usually the binding
architectural constraint in urban FTTH.


================================================================================
4. FIBRE TYPES, CABLES AND STANDARDS
================================================================================

4.1 Single-Mode Fibre Categories

G.652 - Standard single-mode fibre. Zero dispersion near 1310 nm. The .D
variant is low-water-peak and is the default for essentially all new terrestrial
and access construction. If a network document says "standard fibre" without
qualification, it means G.652.D.

G.653 - Dispersion-shifted fibre. Zero dispersion at 1550 nm. Deployed in the
late 1980s, then found to be hostile to DWDM because of four-wave mixing.
Significant installed base in Japan and parts of Italy; a constraint rather
than an asset today.

G.654 - Cut-off shifted fibre with large effective area and very low
attenuation, around 0.16 dB/km. Originally a submarine fibre; the .E variant is
now used for terrestrial long-haul where the larger effective area raises the
nonlinear threshold and buys OSNR margin for high-order modulation.

G.655 - Non-zero dispersion-shifted fibre. A small controlled dispersion at
1550 nm, enough to suppress FWM while limiting accumulation. Widely deployed in
long-haul during the late 1990s and 2000s. Coherent detection reduced its
advantage, and new builds generally revert to G.652.D or G.654.E.

G.656 - Non-zero dispersion for wideband transport across S, C and L bands.

G.657 - Bend-insensitive single-mode fibre, the access workhorse. The .A1 and
.A2 variants remain fully G.652.D compatible and splice to it without penalty;
.B2 and .B3 tolerate bend radii down to 5 mm at the cost of full
interoperability. Bend insensitivity is what makes in-building riser work,
drop cable stapling and dense fibre management in street cabinets practical.

4.2 Multimode Categories

OM1 (62.5/125) and OM2 (50/125) are legacy. OM3, OM4 and OM5 are laser-
optimised 50/125 fibres characterised by effective modal bandwidth: OM3 to
300 m at 10 Gbit/s, OM4 to 400 m, OM5 adding wideband support for short-
wavelength division multiplexing. Relevant to carriers mainly inside central
offices, exchanges and data centres.

4.3 Cable Construction

The fibre is 125 micrometres of glass with a 250 micrometre acrylate coating.
Everything else is packaging designed to keep the glass in a benign mechanical
and chemical environment for 25-40 years.

Loose tube. Fibres float in gel-filled or dry-blocked tubes stranded around a
central strength member. Thermal expansion and cable strain are absorbed by the
tube, not the fibre. The standard outside-plant construction for duct and
aerial.

Ribbon. Twelve or more fibres bonded in a flat array, allowing mass fusion
splicing of an entire ribbon in one operation. Essential for very high fibre
counts. Rollable or "intermittently bonded" ribbon flexes into a round
cross-section and has become the dominant choice for high-density urban and
data centre routes, supporting 1728, 3456 and higher fibre counts in ducts
sized for far less.

Micro-duct and blown fibre. Small-diameter cables installed by air-assisted
blowing into pre-laid micro-ducts. Reduces civil works, enables incremental
build, and is central to modern FTTH deployment economics.

Aerial. ADSS (all-dielectric self-supporting) for spans between poles with no
metallic elements - important where the cable shares structure with power
conductors. OPGW (optical ground wire) integrates fibres into the earth
conductor of a transmission line and is how many utility fibre networks came
into existence.

Drop cable. The final span to the subscriber. Flat, small, bend-insensitive
G.657, often with a pre-connectorised end to eliminate field splicing on the
most cost-sensitive part of the build.

4.4 Connectors and Terminations

Connector loss and reflectance both matter; reflectance matters more than
engineers new to optics expect, because reflections destabilise lasers and
degrade high-order modulation.

Polish types: PC (physical contact, around -40 dB return loss), UPC (ultra
physical contact, around -50 dB), APC (angled physical contact, 8 degree angle,
better than -60 dB). PON plant is APC throughout, because the RF video overlay
and the burst-mode upstream are both intolerant of reflections. Mating an APC
to a UPC connector damages both ferrules and produces high loss - a recurring
field failure.

Form factors: SC (still the access standard, especially SC/APC), LC (duplex,
the transport and data centre standard), MPO/MTP (multi-fibre, 12 to 24 fibres,
used for parallel optics and high-density patching), and field-installable
mechanical connectors for drop terminations.

5. CARRIER NETWORK ARCHITECTURE
================================================================================

5.1 The Layered View

A carrier optical network is conventionally decomposed into layers, and
confusion in design discussions almost always traces to people speaking about
different layers without saying so.

Layer 0 - the optical or photonic layer. Fibre, amplifiers, wavelength
multiplexers, ROADMs. Deals in wavelengths and analogue optical performance:
power, OSNR, dispersion, nonlinearity.

Layer 1 - the digital transport layer. OTN (ITU-T G.709), providing
containerised transport with forward error correction, tandem connection
monitoring and deterministic multiplexing. In some architectures Ethernet is
carried directly over the wavelength and Layer 1 collapses into the transceiver.

Layer 2 - Ethernet, including carrier Ethernet services defined by MEF, VLAN
and PBB structures, and segment routing constructs where used.

Layer 3 - IP/MPLS routing.

The decades-long architectural argument is how much function to place in each
layer. The "IP over DWDM" position holds that OTN is redundant overhead when
routers can host coherent pluggables and drive wavelengths directly. The
transport position holds that OTN provides multiplexing efficiency, hard
service isolation, and an operational demarcation that survives router
software failures and organisational boundaries. In practice large carriers run
hybrids, and the balance has shifted towards IP over DWDM as pluggable coherent
matured.

5.2 Domains

Access. From the subscriber to the first aggregation point. Dominated by PON
and point-to-point Ethernet. Distances typically 1-20 km. Cost per subscriber
is the governing metric.

Aggregation and metro. Rings or partial meshes connecting access nodes to metro
core sites. 10-100 km spans. Increasingly coherent, with 100G/400G pluggables
replacing grey optics and dedicated transponders. This is where mobile
transport, business services and residential backhaul converge onto common
infrastructure.

Regional and long-haul core. 100-2000+ km. C or C+L band DWDM with EDFA and
Raman amplification, ROADM nodes, coherent transponders or high-performance
pluggables at 400G to 1.6T per wavelength.

Submarine. Repeatered systems with undersea EDFAs powered over a copper
conductor in the cable, spaced 50-100 km, with the shore-side power feed
equipment providing several thousand volts. Space-division multiplexed designs
with many fibre pairs at moderate per-pair capacity have become preferred to
fewer pairs at maximum capacity, because the binding constraint is electrical
power delivered to the repeaters, not spectral efficiency.

Data centre interconnect. Short, extremely high capacity, latency- and
power-sensitive. The domain where pluggable coherent first displaced
transponders, and the one currently growing fastest under AI workloads.

5.3 Topologies and Protection

Point-to-point is the simplest and least resilient. A single fibre cut takes
the service down.

Ring topologies dominate metro and aggregation because they provide a second
path with modest incremental fibre. Protection can be at the optical layer
(optical line protection switching, typically 50 ms), at the OTN layer
(SNCP/subnetwork connection protection), at Layer 2, or at Layer 3. Carriers
must avoid stacking protection mechanisms that race each other: a well-known
operational failure mode is optical protection and IP fast-reroute both firing
on the same event and producing extended instability.

Mesh topologies are the norm in the core. Restoration is computed rather than
pre-provisioned - a controller finds a new path and re-tunes wavelengths after
a failure. This trades restoration time (seconds rather than milliseconds) for
much better capacity efficiency, because spare capacity is shared across many
failure scenarios rather than dedicated per circuit. Colourless, directionless
and contentionless ROADM architectures exist specifically to make this possible.

In the access network, protection is usually absent for residential service -
the drop and the distribution fibre are single points of failure and the
economics do not support duplicating them. Business and mobile transport
services over PON can be protected using Type B protection (redundant feeder
fibre and OLT port, shared splitter) or Type C (fully duplicated path to the
ONU). Type B is the common compromise, protecting the shared segment where a
failure would affect many customers.

5.4 Wavelength Planning

Fixed grid: ITU-T G.694.1 defines a 50 GHz (and legacy 100 GHz) grid anchored
at 193.1 THz. Simple, and adequate while all channels were the same width.

Flexible grid: also G.694.1, defining 12.5 GHz granularity so that a channel
can occupy the spectrum it actually needs. Necessary once 400G and 800G signals
at high baud rates exceeded 50 GHz. Introduces spectrum fragmentation as a
planning problem analogous to memory fragmentation, which is one of the
practical drivers for controller-based path computation.

Coarse WDM (G.694.2) uses 20 nm spacing across 1270-1610 nm, requiring only
uncooled lasers. Cheap, low channel count, and still widely used for mobile
fronthaul and business access where 8-16 channels suffice.

5.5 Mobile Transport: Fronthaul, Midhaul, Backhaul

The disaggregation of the radio access network into radio unit, distributed
unit and centralised unit created new transport segments with sharply different
requirements, and made optical access engineering directly relevant to mobile
network design.

Fronthaul carries the eCPRI or CPRI interface between the radio unit and the
distributed unit. It is the most demanding segment: latency budget of roughly
100 microseconds one way, which limits distance to about 20 km; bandwidth of
several gigabits to tens of gigabits per radio; and stringent synchronisation.
Options include dedicated dark fibre, CWDM or DWDM over a shared fibre,
point-to-point 25 Gbit/s grey optics, and increasingly PON-based fronthaul
where a 25G or 50G PON carries fronthaul alongside residential traffic using
cooperative dynamic bandwidth assignment to protect the latency budget.

Midhaul connects distributed unit to centralised unit. Latency budget in the
low milliseconds, considerably more relaxed.

Backhaul connects to the core. Conventional IP transport requirements, with
capacity per site now commonly 10 Gbit/s and rising.

Synchronisation deserves separate attention. Time-division duplex 5G requires
phase alignment typically within 1.5 microseconds relative to a common
reference, and some coordinated features demand tighter. This is delivered
using Precision Time Protocol (IEEE 1588v2) with telecom profiles G.8275.1 or
G.8275.2, and Synchronous Ethernet (G.8262) for frequency. Every optical node
in the chain must either be PTP-aware or have deterministic, symmetric delay.
Asymmetric paths - different fibre lengths in each direction, or unidirectional
amplifiers - introduce time error that is difficult to diagnose after the fact.
This is a frequent source of field problems when transport teams and radio
teams do not coordinate.

6. THE ACCESS NETWORK IN DEPTH: PON AND POINT-TO-POINT
================================================================================

6.1 FTTx Taxonomy

    FTTH   Fibre to the home       Fibre terminates inside the dwelling
    FTTB   Fibre to the building   Terminates in the basement; copper or
                                   in-building Ethernet/G.fast to units
    FTTC   Fibre to the cabinet    Street cabinet, VDSL2 over the last 300 m
    FTTN   Fibre to the node       Larger node, longer copper tail
    FTTdp  Fibre to the distribution point  Very short copper, G.fast
    FTTP   Fibre to the premises   Generic; covers FTTH and FTTB

The industry has converged on FTTH/FTTP for new build because copper-tail
architectures require active powered cabinets in the field, which carry
recurring power, battery, maintenance and site-rental costs, and because their
capacity ceiling arrives quickly.

6.2 PON Principle of Operation

A PON is a point-to-multipoint tree. The optical line terminal sits in the
central office or a remote node. A feeder fibre runs to one or more passive
optical splitters, which divide the signal among distribution fibres, each
ending at an optical network unit or optical network terminal at the customer.

Downstream is a broadcast. The OLT transmits continuously; every ONU receives
every frame and discards those not addressed to it. Confidentiality is provided
by AES encryption keyed per ONU.

Upstream is time-division multiple access. Only one ONU may transmit at any
instant, or the bursts collide at the splitter. The OLT grants each ONU
transmission windows. Because ONUs are at different distances, their signals
would arrive at different times and with different power levels, so two
mechanisms are essential:

Ranging. During activation the OLT measures round-trip delay to each ONU and
assigns an equalisation delay so that all ONUs appear equidistant. This is why
PON standards specify a maximum differential distance (typically 20 km) as well
as a maximum reach.

Burst-mode reception. The OLT receiver must lock onto each burst's amplitude
and clock phase within a few hundred nanoseconds, across a dynamic range of
15-20 dB between near and far ONUs. Burst-mode receiver design is one of the
genuinely hard engineering problems in PON and a significant part of OLT cost.

Dynamic bandwidth assignment allocates upstream capacity. Status-reporting DBA
uses ONU queue reports; non-status-reporting DBA infers demand from idle
patterns. Service level agreements are expressed as T-CONT types: fixed,
assured, non-assured and best-effort bandwidth, composed to create business and
residential product tiers on the same PON.

6.3 Generational Comparison

    Standard      Spec         Down      Up        Wavelengths (nm)
    ---------     ---------    -------   -------   --------------------------
    GPON          G.984        2.488G    1.244G    D 1490, U 1310
    EPON          802.3ah      1.25G     1.25G     D 1490, U 1310
    10G-EPON      802.3av      10G       1G/10G    D 1577, U 1270
    XG-PON1       G.987        10G       2.5G      D 1577, U 1270
    XGS-PON       G.9807       10G       10G       D 1577, U 1270
    NG-PON2       G.989        4x10G     4x10G     Tunable, 1596-1603 down
    25GS-PON      MSA          25G       25G       D 1358, U 1290 (typical)
    50G PON       G.9804       50G       25G/50G   D 1342, U 1290/1300 region
    -----------------------------------------------------------------------
    RF video overlay, where present, occupies 1550-1560 nm

Coexistence is the central deployment consideration. GPON, XGS-PON and an RF
video overlay can share the same fibre plant simultaneously via a wavelength
coexistence element at the OLT, allowing subscriber-by-subscriber migration
without re-engineering the outside plant. The wavelength plan for 50G PON has
been the subject of extended standards work precisely because the O band is
crowded and triple coexistence with both GPON and XGS-PON is commercially
important to operators with mixed installed bases.

6.4 The Optical Distribution Network

The ODN is the passive plant: feeder fibre, splitters, distribution fibre,
drops, and the closures, cabinets and terminals that house them. It represents
70-80 percent of the capital cost of an FTTH build and has a design life of
30 years or more. Active equipment will be replaced two or three times over
the life of the ODN, so ODN design decisions are the most consequential and
least reversible in access engineering.

Split architecture choices:

Centralised splitting. A single split stage, usually at the central office or
in a large street cabinet. Simplifies operations and testing, maximises
flexibility in assigning subscribers to OLT ports, and makes it easy to change
split ratios later. Consumes more distribution fibre.

Distributed or cascaded splitting. Two or more stages, for example 1:4 in a
cabinet and 1:8 in a closure near the customer. Minimises fibre in the
distribution segment and is cheaper in low-density areas. Harder to test,
because an OTRD trace beyond the first splitter cannot distinguish branches.

Split ratio selection. 1:32 and 1:64 are the common choices. The trade-off is
direct: a higher split ratio shares the OLT port and feeder fibre across more
subscribers, lowering cost per home, but reduces both the optical power budget
and the average bandwidth available per subscriber. With XGS-PON, a 1:64 split
gives a nominal 156 Mbit/s per subscriber if all transmit simultaneously, which
is acceptable given real-world concurrency but constrains gigabit product
marketing. Business and mobile transport services typically justify lower split
ratios or dedicated wavelengths.

A worked power budget for XGS-PON Class N1 (29 dB budget), 1:32 centralised
split, 8 km reach:

    Feeder + distribution fibre, 8 km at 0.35 dB/km (1310 nm)   2.8 dB
    1:32 splitter including excess loss                        17.5 dB
    Connectors, 6 pairs at 0.3 dB                               1.8 dB
    Splices, 8 at 0.1 dB                                        0.8 dB
    Coexistence element                                         1.0 dB
    ------------------------------------------------------------------
    Total                                                      23.9 dB
    Budget                                                     29.0 dB
    Remaining margin                                            5.1 dB

The margin looks comfortable, but note that at 1:64 the splitter alone costs
about 20.5 dB and the same route leaves under 2 dB - below the 3 dB repair
allowance most operators mandate. This single calculation drives most urban
split-ratio policy.

6.5 Point-to-Point Active Ethernet

Each subscriber gets a dedicated fibre to an Ethernet aggregation switch. It
offers full dedicated bandwidth, simple troubleshooting, clean unbundling at
the fibre level, and no shared-medium security considerations. It costs
substantially more in feeder fibre count, in central office space, and in port
count and power. It remains the architecture of choice in parts of Sweden and
the Netherlands, in some municipal networks, and for enterprise and mobile
sites even within PON-based operators.

Regulatory context matters here. In markets where wholesale access is mandated
at the physical layer, point-to-point makes fibre unbundling straightforward.
Where wholesale is provided as a bitstream service, PON is sufficient. National
regulatory choices have therefore shaped architecture as much as engineering
economics has.

6.6 PON for Business and Transport

PON is no longer a residential-only technology. Carriers use it for:

Small and medium business connectivity, with symmetric XGS-PON delivering
service levels previously requiring dedicated fibre at a fraction of the cost.

Mobile xHaul, using dedicated T-CONTs, low-latency DBA and PTP-aware ONUs to
carry midhaul and, with careful engineering, fronthaul.

Wholesale and open access, where the PON operator sells a bitstream product to
retail service providers.

Multi-tenant and campus networks, replacing in-building Ethernet switch
hierarchies with passive splitters - lower power, less space, longer reach, at
the cost of a different operational model.

7. HARDWARE
================================================================================

7.1 Sources

Fabry-Perot laser diode. Multi-longitudinal-mode, broad spectrum, cheap.
Adequate for short reach at low rates. Used in legacy ONU upstream transmitters.

Distributed feedback (DFB) laser. An internal Bragg grating selects a single
longitudinal mode, giving narrow linewidth and side-mode suppression above
35 dB. The workhorse for PON, CWDM and grey optics.

Distributed Bragg reflector and tunable lasers. Tunable across the C band,
required for NG-PON2 ONUs, for colourless ROADM architectures and for coherent
transponders where the same module must be provisioned to any channel. Tunable
optics cost was the principal reason NG-PON2 failed to reach mass market.

External cavity and integrable tunable laser assemblies (ITLA). Very narrow
linewidth, typically under 100 kHz, required as both signal source and local
oscillator in coherent systems. Linewidth directly limits achievable modulation
order: 64QAM demands far more phase stability than QPSK.

Vertical-cavity surface-emitting lasers (VCSEL). Emit perpendicular to the
wafer surface, enabling on-wafer testing and very low cost. Dominant in
multimode short reach; long-wavelength VCSELs have made inroads into
single-mode access.

Modulation. Direct modulation - varying the drive current - is cheap but
produces chirp, a dynamic wavelength shift that interacts badly with chromatic
dispersion. External modulation using an electro-absorption modulator
(compact, integrable, moderate performance) or a Mach-Zehnder modulator in
lithium niobate, indium phosphide or thin-film lithium niobate (high
performance, required for coherent and high baud rates) avoids chirp and
enables arbitrary complex constellations.

7.2 Detectors and Receivers

PIN photodiode. Simple, robust, no internal gain. Used where power is adequate.

Avalanche photodiode (APD). Internal multiplication gain of 10-20, improving
sensitivity by 5-10 dB at the cost of excess noise, temperature-dependent bias
and higher price. Standard in PON OLT and ONU receivers where the power budget
is tight.

Burst-mode receiver. An OLT-specific APD front end with fast automatic gain
control and clock and data recovery that settles within the preamble of each
upstream burst. Its dynamic range determines how much near-far power difference
the PON can tolerate.

Coherent receiver. A 90-degree optical hybrid mixes signal and local oscillator
to produce in-phase and quadrature components for both polarisations, feeding
four balanced photodiode pairs and four high-speed ADCs. Everything after that
is digital signal processing: chromatic dispersion compensation, polarisation
demultiplexing and PMD tracking via adaptive equalisation, carrier frequency
and phase recovery, symbol decision, and soft-decision forward error correction.
The DSP ASIC is the most complex and valuable component in modern optical
transport, and its process node and power envelope largely determine what form
factor a given capacity can fit into.

7.3 Amplification

Erbium-doped fibre amplifier (EDFA). A length of erbium-doped fibre pumped at
980 nm or 1480 nm. Provides 20-30 dB of gain across the C band with a noise
figure around 4-5 dB. It amplifies all wavelengths simultaneously and is
transparent to bit rate and modulation format - the property that made DWDM
economic. Variants include L-band EDFAs, gain-flattened designs, and
gain-clamped amplifiers that hold per-channel power stable as channels are
added or dropped.

Raman amplifier. Pumps the transmission fibre itself at a wavelength about
100 nm below the signal, producing distributed gain along the span rather than
lumped gain at a node. Because amplification occurs before the signal has fully
decayed, effective noise figure is better - sometimes quoted as negative when
referred to an equivalent lumped amplifier. Used to extend spans, in
high-capacity and submarine systems, and to manage C+L tilt. Requires high pump
power and careful safety interlocking.

Semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA). Compact and integrable, with fast gain
dynamics that cause crosstalk in WDM but are useful for switching and for burst
applications. Increasingly used in PON to extend reach and split ratio, and in
integrated photonic circuits.

Optical amplifiers do not regenerate: they amplify noise along with signal and
add amplified spontaneous emission. OSNR degrades monotonically along an
amplified chain, and the number of spans is bounded by the receiver's required
OSNR. Regeneration - optical-electrical-optical conversion - resets the budget
but is expensive in cost, power and latency, which is why coherent's OSNR
tolerance translates directly into fewer regenerator sites and lower operating
cost.

7.4 Multiplexing, Filtering and Switching

Arrayed waveguide grating (AWG). A planar device that separates or combines
wavelengths using a phased array of waveguides. The standard passive
multiplexer for DWDM and the core of many PON coexistence elements.

Thin-film filters. Cascaded dielectric filters for CWDM and low-channel-count
applications. Cheap and stable.

Wavelength selective switch (WSS). The heart of a ROADM. Using liquid crystal
on silicon or MEMS micromirrors, a WSS routes any wavelength - or, in flexible
grid designs, any spectral slice - from any input port to any output port, and
applies per-channel attenuation for power equalisation. A 1xN WSS with N of 9
to 32 is typical.

ROADM architecture terminology, which is worth being precise about because it
determines what the network can do without a truck roll:

  Colourless      Any add/drop port can carry any wavelength (tunable optics,
                  no fixed filter per port).
  Directionless   Any add/drop port can be routed towards any line direction.
  Contentionless  Two channels on the same wavelength can be added on
                  different directions from the same add/drop structure.
  Gridless        Supports flexible grid spectral allocation.

A CDC-F ROADM (colourless, directionless, contentionless, flexible grid) allows
a controller to establish or restore a wavelength between any two nodes purely
in software. It costs more per node than a fixed or directional design, and the
business case rests on restoration efficiency and on service velocity rather
than on first-install cost.

Optical circuit switching, historically confined to fibre-level patch
automation, has re-entered mainstream discussion through AI data centre
topologies, where reconfigurable optical interconnect between accelerator
clusters offers power savings over electrical switching.

7.5 Transceivers and Form Factors

The industry has moved decisively from embedded, vendor-specific line cards to
standardised pluggable modules.

    Form factor   Typical capacity        Notes
    -----------   --------------------    ------------------------------
    SFP           155M - 1G               Legacy access, PON ONU
    SFP+          10G                     XGS-PON, grey optics
    SFP28         25G                     Fronthaul, 25G PON
    QSFP28        100G                    4x25G lanes
    QSFP-DD       400G, 800G              8 lanes; hosts 400ZR/ZR+
    OSFP          400G, 800G, 1.6T        Better thermal envelope
    CFP2-DCO      100G - 400G coherent    Digital coherent optics
    OSFP-XD /     1.6T                    Emerging high-density
      QSFP-DD800

The significant architectural shift is coherent-in-a-pluggable. OIF's 400ZR
implementation agreement defined a 400 Gbit/s coherent interface that fits a
QSFP-DD and runs on data centre power budgets, targeting unamplified and
amplified DCI reach. OpenZR+ extended reach and added OTN framing options.
800ZR and ZR+ followed and are now shipping in volume, extending pluggable
coherent beyond metro into regional and selected long-haul applications, and
OIF has published the 1600ZR implementation agreement taking a single
wavelength to 1.6 Tbit/s.

The consequence for carriers is structural. A coherent pluggable in a router
port removes a transponder shelf, its chassis, its power, its rack space and
its management system. It also removes a demarcation point, merges two
operational domains that in many carriers are separate departments with
separate tools and separate fault processes, and creates multi-vendor
interoperability questions at the optical layer that the OTN-based model
avoided. The technology decision is straightforward; the organisational one is
usually not.

7.6 Access Node Hardware

Optical line terminal (OLT). A chassis or pizza-box platform hosting PON line
cards, uplink cards and switching fabric. Key selection criteria: PON ports per
card and per chassis, supported PON generations on the same card, per-port and
aggregate throughput, uplink capacity (increasingly 100G and 400G), DBA
behaviour under load, power and cooling per subscriber, and support for
disaggregated software models. The distinction between chassis-based OLTs in
central offices and temperature-hardened remote OLTs in cabinets or even on
poles is increasingly important as operators push fibre deeper.

Optical network terminal/unit (ONT/ONU). The subscriber device. Ranges from a
simple single-port bridge to an integrated gateway with Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7,
voice ports and routing. Important considerations are management model (OMCI,
plus TR-069 or the newer TR-369/USP for the gateway function), power
consumption at scale, and forward compatibility with the next PON generation.

Passive splitters. Planar lightwave circuit splitters are the standard,
providing even split across all ports; fused biconical taper splitters allow
uneven ratios for tapping architectures. Splitters are often the only component
in the ODN that engineers have latitude to re-specify late in the design.

Enclosures. Joint closures, fibre distribution hubs, street cabinets, building
entry terminals and customer terminals. Unglamorous, and the source of a
disproportionate share of field faults through water ingress, poor bend
management and incorrect re-entry during subsequent works.

7.7 Test and Measurement

Optical time domain reflectometer (OTDR). Launches a pulse and measures
backscatter and reflection against time, producing a distance-resolved trace of
the fibre. Locates breaks, high-loss splices and reflective events. On PON
plant, an OTDR launched from the OLT side cannot resolve individual branches
beyond a splitter, because all branches overlay in the same time window. This
is addressed by testing from the subscriber side, by using reflective
terminations with distinguishable signatures, or by embedded monitoring at
1650 nm using filtered WDM couplers that keep the test wavelength out of the
service bands.

Optical power meter and light source. The basic insertion-loss measurement.
Bidirectional loss testing is the acceptance standard for new plant.

Optical spectrum analyser. Channel power, OSNR, wavelength accuracy. Essential
for DWDM commissioning.

Fusion splicer. Core-alignment splicers achieve 0.02-0.05 dB routinely; ribbon
splicers handle 12 fibres at once. Splice quality is the most common variable
between a plant that meets budget and one that does not.

Fibre inspection scope. Video microscopy of connector end faces against
IEC 61300-3-35 cleanliness grades. Contamination on connectors is, by a
significant margin, the most common cause of optical faults in service. Any
disciplined operations practice inspects before every mating.

8. SOFTWARE, CONTROL AND MANAGEMENT
================================================================================

8.1 The Management Stack

    Business support systems (BSS)
      Billing, ordering, product catalogue, customer records
    ------------------------------------------------------------
    Operations support systems (OSS)
      Inventory, service activation and fulfilment, assurance,
      workforce management, GIS and outside plant records
    ------------------------------------------------------------
    Domain orchestration / SDN controller
      Multi-vendor path computation, service provisioning,
      restoration, policy
    ------------------------------------------------------------
    Element / network management system (EMS/NMS)
      Vendor-specific configuration, alarms, performance
    ------------------------------------------------------------
    Network elements
      OLT, ONU, ROADM, amplifier, transponder

Historically each vendor supplied a proprietary EMS, and a carrier with three
optical vendors ran three management silos with three fault workflows. The
sustained industry effort of the last decade has been to push standardised,
model-driven interfaces down to the element so that a single controller can
manage a heterogeneous network.

8.2 Model-Driven Networking

NETCONF (RFC 6241) and the newer RESTCONF provide transaction-oriented
configuration with candidate datastores, commit and rollback semantics - a
meaningful improvement over SNMP and CLI scraping, which offered no
transactionality. gNMI, from the OpenConfig community, provides efficient
streaming telemetry and configuration over gRPC.

YANG (RFC 7950) is the data modelling language. The competing model ecosystems
matter in procurement:

  OpenConfig      Operator-authored, vendor-neutral, strong in IP and
                  increasingly in optical terminal devices.
  OpenROADM       An operator-led MSA defining interoperable ROADM, pluggable
                  and transponder models plus optical specifications, so that
                  equipment from different vendors interworks on one line
                  system.
  ONF TAPI        Transport API - a northbound interface abstracting an optical
                  domain to an orchestrator as topology, connectivity and
                  path computation services.
  IETF models     ietf-te-topology, ietf-optical-impairment-topology and
                  related models for the ACTN framework.
  BBF models      Access-specific: cloud CO, disaggregated OLT, PON management.

Telemetry has shifted from polled SNMP counters at five-minute intervals to
streaming subscriptions at sub-second granularity. This changes what is
analytically possible: pre-FEC bit error rate, received optical power and
polarisation state sampled continuously allow degradation to be detected before
it causes an alarm.

8.3 SDN in the Optical Domain

The optical layer resisted SDN longer than the packet layer, for a physical
reason: a wavelength path is not merely a logical construct. Whether it works
depends on OSNR, accumulated dispersion, filter narrowing at each ROADM,
nonlinear penalty and the modulation format chosen. A controller that computes
paths without a physical impairment model will provision circuits that do not
come up.

Modern optical controllers therefore incorporate a physical layer model - often
based on the open-source GNPy library from the Telecom Infra Project - to
perform impairment-aware routing, modulation and spectrum assignment. The
controller answers not "is there a free channel" but "is there a free channel
on which 800G 64QAM will close with adequate margin, and if not, will 400G
16QAM close instead".

Capabilities this enables: automated wavelength provisioning in minutes rather
than weeks; mesh restoration computed after failure instead of pre-provisioned;
margin harvesting, where links initially provisioned conservatively are
upgraded to higher modulation orders once real performance data accumulates;
and multi-layer optimisation, where IP topology and optical topology are
planned together rather than sequentially.

8.4 Disaggregation

Three related but distinct movements:

Hardware/software disaggregation at the network element. The SDN-enabled
Broadband Access (SEBA) and Virtual OLT Hardware Abstraction (VOLTHA) projects,
originating in ONF and continued through operator communities, define a model
where a white-box OLT runs standardised software and is controlled through an
abstraction layer, allowing the OLT hardware and the management software to be
sourced separately. Telecom Infra Project's work on disaggregated access and
transport pursues similar ends.

Optical line system disaggregation. Separating the open line system - fibre,
amplifiers, ROADMs - from the transponders that light it, so that a carrier can
introduce a new generation of coherent optics without replacing the line
system, and can source them from different vendors. This is the model that
pluggable coherent makes natural, and it is where OpenROADM and OpenConfig
terminal models earn their keep.

Cloud central office. Re-hosting broadband network gateway, subscriber
management and related functions as software on commodity compute in edge data
centres rather than on purpose-built chassis. Combines with access node
disaggregation to turn the central office into a small cloud site.

Disaggregation transfers integration risk from the vendor to the operator.
Carriers that have succeeded with it invested substantially in internal systems
engineering, test automation and lab capability. Those that treated it purely
as a procurement lever generally did not achieve the savings they modelled.

8.5 Access-Specific Management

ONU Management and Control Interface (OMCI, ITU-T G.988) is the in-band
protocol by which the OLT configures and monitors the ONU - service profiles,
VLANs, T-CONTs, GEM ports, performance counters and alarms. It is
comprehensive, deeply structured, and a frequent source of interoperability
friction: two vendors can both be compliant and still fail to interwork because
they model a service differently within the permitted freedom of the standard.
Broadband Forum TR-247 and TR-255 conformance testing exists specifically to
reduce this.

TR-069 (CWMP) and its successor TR-369 (User Services Platform) manage the
residential gateway function above the ONU - firmware, Wi-Fi configuration,
diagnostics. TR-369 replaces the polling, single-controller CWMP model with a
publish-subscribe architecture supporting multiple controllers, which matters
for operators running managed Wi-Fi and third-party service platforms.

Broadband Forum TR-101, TR-156, TR-167 and TR-178 define the architectural
frameworks for Ethernet-based aggregation and PON access; TR-384 and the cloud
CO work define the virtualised equivalents.

8.6 Planning, Design and GIS Tooling

Outside plant design is a geospatial optimisation problem, and the software
that supports it has become a genuine competitive differentiator in FTTH
rollouts. A modern toolchain ingests cadastral and building data, existing duct
and pole records, and traffic forecasts, then generates candidate network
designs, computes bills of material, estimates civil works cost per premise and
produces splice schedules and as-built documentation. Automated design can cut
engineering time per premise by an order of magnitude relative to manual CAD
work, which matters when a national build covers millions of premises.

The quality of the underlying records is the limiting factor. Carriers with
accurate duct and pole inventory build faster and cheaper than those without,
and a significant share of FTTH cost overruns trace to discovering that the
duct is collapsed, occupied or absent.

8.7 Analytics, Machine Learning and Digital Twins

Applied uses that are in production rather than aspirational:

Predictive fibre fault detection. Trending OTDR signatures, received optical
power and pre-FEC BER to identify degrading splices, water ingress and bend
events before service is affected.

Optical margin optimisation. Using measured performance to reclaim design
margin, increasing modulation order or channel count on existing links.

PON performance analytics. Correlating ONU-reported statistics across a
population to distinguish a plant fault affecting one branch from a Wi-Fi
problem inside one home - an important cost driver, since a large fraction of
broadband truck rolls address faults that are not in the operator's network.

Traffic forecasting and capacity planning, feeding augmentation decisions on
PON ports, uplinks and wavelengths.

Field workforce optimisation, routing technicians and predicting job duration.

Digital twin of the optical network. A simulation model kept synchronised with
live telemetry, used to test configuration changes, evaluate failure scenarios
and validate upgrade plans before touching production. The value depends
entirely on inventory accuracy; a twin built on stale records produces
confident wrong answers.

Distributed fibre optic sensing deserves mention as an emerging software-
plus-hardware capability. Interrogating the fibre itself for acoustic,
temperature or strain signatures turns the cable into a distributed sensor,
supporting third-party dig detection, cable route intrusion monitoring and
even seismic and infrastructure monitoring as an ancillary revenue stream.


================================================================================
9. DEPLOYMENT, TEST AND OPERATIONS
================================================================================

9.1 Where the Money Goes

In a typical FTTH build, civil works - trenching, ducting, pole make-ready,
permits, reinstatement - account for 60-80 percent of capital cost. The glass
itself is a minor line item. Every architectural decision that reduces digging
therefore has more financial leverage than any decision about optics.

This drives the standard cost-reduction playbook: reuse existing ducts and
poles wherever regulation permits; micro-trenching and narrow-trench techniques
in urban streets; aerial deployment where the pole infrastructure exists and
local rules allow; directional boring under obstacles; and micro-duct with
blown fibre so that the expensive civil pass is made once and capacity is added
later without re-excavation.

Pole and duct access regulation is consequently a first-order commercial
variable, not a legal footnote. Markets that mandated cost-based access to
incumbent ducts and poles have seen materially faster and cheaper fibre
rollouts than those that did not.

9.2 Build Sequence

  1. Survey and design. Desktop design from GIS records, then field validation.
     The gap between records and reality is where schedules slip.
  2. Permitting and wayleaves. Frequently the longest-duration activity, and
     largely outside engineering control.
  3. Civil works. Duct, chambers, pole make-ready.
  4. Cable installation. Blowing or pulling, with tension and bend radius
     monitored - fibre damaged during installation may pass initial test and
     fail months later.
  5. Splicing and termination. Closures, cabinets, splitter installation.
  6. Test and acceptance. Bidirectional insertion loss and OTDR traces on every
     fibre, recorded against the as-built record.
  7. As-built documentation. The step most often compressed under schedule
     pressure and the one that most reliably generates cost later.
  8. Service activation. Drop installation, ONT provisioning, in-home work.

9.3 Acceptance Criteria

Typical thresholds for carrier acceptance of new access plant:

    Fusion splice loss                 <= 0.10 dB (bidirectional average)
    Connector pair loss                <= 0.35 dB
    Connector return loss (APC)        <= -55 dB
    End-to-end ODN loss                Within design budget + 1 dB
    No reflective events above         -45 dB in the path
    Fibre end face cleanliness         IEC 61300-3-35 compliant

Bidirectional measurement matters because OTDR traces are asymmetric where mode
field diameters differ; a splice between dissimilar fibres can read as gain in
one direction and excess loss in the other. Averaging both directions gives the
true figure. Technicians who report "gainers" have usually not been trained on
this.

9.4 Fault Management in Live Networks

The fault categories, in rough order of frequency:

Customer premises and in-home Wi-Fi issues. Not a fibre fault at all, but they
consume network operations attention and generate truck rolls. Distinguishing
them cheaply and remotely is one of the highest-return investments in access
operations.

Drop cable damage. Pets, gardening, building work, ladders. Localised and
cheap to fix but high in volume.

Connector contamination and poor re-mating, typically introduced during other
maintenance activity.

Distribution or feeder fibre cuts. Third-party excavation is the dominant
cause worldwide. A feeder cut affects everyone behind it, which is why feeder
routes are the segment where diverse routing and protection can be justified.

Splitter and closure degradation, usually water ingress over years.

Active equipment failure - the least common category in a well-run network,
which is a reversal of the copper-era intuition.

Mean time to repair on outside plant is dominated by locate-and-access time,
not by splicing time. Accurate records, embedded monitoring and pre-positioned
spares move the number more than technician skill does.

9.5 Safety

Optical power at the levels used in amplified DWDM and Raman systems is
genuinely hazardous to eyesight and, at Raman pump levels, capable of igniting
contamination on a connector end face. Fibre ends are also a puncture and
ingestion hazard. Standard practice: never view a fibre end with the naked eye
or an unfiltered scope, treat every fibre as live until proven otherwise, use
automatic laser shutdown (ITU-T G.664) on amplified systems, dispose of fibre
offcuts into a dedicated container, and observe the IEC 60825 laser safety
classification applicable to the equipment.

10. BUSINESS USE CASES
================================================================================

10.1 Residential Broadband

The volume case. Fibre displaces DSL, cable and fixed wireless on the strength
of symmetric capacity, latency stability, and an operating cost structure that
is fundamentally better: no field-powered cabinets, no line conditioning, far
fewer faults, and lower energy per subscriber.

The commercial arguments an operator actually makes:

  - Lower churn. Fibre subscribers churn measurably less than DSL subscribers
    in most markets, which compounds over the asset life.
  - Lower cost to serve. Fewer faults per subscriber per year, fewer truck
    rolls, lower energy.
  - Higher ARPU through speed tiering, though the elasticity is weak: most
    households do not use a gigabit, and the marketing challenge is to sell
    reliability and upstream capacity rather than headline speed.
  - Adjacent revenue: managed Wi-Fi, security, streaming bundles, smart home.
  - Asset value. Passed and connected homes on fibre are valued by
    infrastructure investors at multiples that copper networks do not attract.

The risk is take-up. A build is justified on homes connected, not homes passed,
and in competitive markets two or three operators may overbuild the same
street, splitting a take rate that none of their business cases assumed.

10.2 Business Connectivity

Three tiers, with different economics:

Dedicated internet access and point-to-point Ethernet over dark or lit fibre,
sold to large enterprises with committed service levels, sub-10 ms latency
guarantees and rapid restoration. High margin, low volume, long sales cycle.

Symmetric business broadband over XGS-PON, addressing the small and medium
business segment that was historically served by leased lines it could not
afford or by consumer broadband that did not meet its needs. This is a
significant volume opportunity because the incremental cost over residential
PON is small while the price premium is large.

Multi-site connectivity, increasingly delivered as SD-WAN over fibre
underlay, where the fibre operator either sells the underlay wholesale or moves
up the stack to sell the managed service.

10.3 Mobile Transport

Fibre and mobile are not separate businesses. Dense 5G, and prospectively 6G,
is unbuildable without fibre to the cell site, and the small-cell densification
required for millimetre-wave and high-band coverage multiplies the number of
sites requiring transport.

For an operator that owns both, this creates a real strategic advantage: the
FTTH build and the cell site backhaul build share routes, share civil works and
share transport equipment. For an operator that owns only mobile, transport
becomes a large recurring cost paid to whoever owns the fibre. This asymmetry
explains a substantial share of the fibre acquisition and joint-venture activity
of recent years.

PON-based xHaul is the notable technical development: reusing the residential
ODN to carry cell site transport, with dedicated wavelengths or dedicated
T-CONTs and low-latency scheduling. It turns an existing asset into mobile
transport at near-zero incremental civil cost.

10.4 Data Centre Interconnect and AI Infrastructure

The fastest-growing transport segment. Requirements differ from classical
carrier transport: very high capacity between a small number of sites, extreme
sensitivity to cost and power per bit, tolerance for simpler line systems, and
a buyer who is technically sophisticated and willing to disaggregate.

AI training and inference have intensified this. Training clusters exceed the
power available at a single site, forcing distributed training across
campuses and driving demand for very high capacity, low latency, tightly
synchronised interconnect over metro and regional distances. Hyperscalers have
responded by buying dark fibre at scale, funding new routes, investing directly
in fibre manufacturing, and pushing optical technology roadmaps in directions
that carriers then inherit.

For carriers the opportunity is wholesale capacity, dark fibre leasing,
colocation adjacency and managed optical fibre networks where the carrier
builds and operates a dedicated line system for a single hyperscaler customer.
The risk is that these customers are few, sophisticated and capable of
building their own.

10.5 Wholesale, Open Access and Dark Fibre

Separating infrastructure from retail service has become a mainstream model.

Dark fibre leasing sells the glass, with the customer providing all optics.
Simple, low-touch, long contract terms, and attractive to financial investors
because revenue is predictable and operating cost is low.

Wavelength services sell a managed lambda - more revenue per fibre than dark
fibre but requiring an operated line system.

Bitstream and open access PON sells a Layer 2 or Layer 3 service to retail
service providers, who own the customer relationship. This is the model behind
national wholesale networks and many altnet builds, and it depends heavily on
regulated or contractual non-discrimination.

The structural trend is that passive infrastructure is increasingly owned by
entities optimising for stable long-term yield, while service competition
happens above it. Engineers should understand that this changes technical
priorities: a wholesale-only operator optimises for open interfaces, service
velocity and operational cost per port, not for differentiated retail features.

10.6 Utilities, Transport and Public Sector

Electricity utilities were among the earliest large fibre owners through OPGW
on transmission lines, originally for protection signalling and SCADA. Many
have monetised surplus capacity, and some have become significant wholesale
carriers. The same is true of railways, motorway authorities and pipeline
operators, all of whom own long linear rights of way - the scarcest asset in
telecoms.

Public sector use cases include municipal networks, national research and
education networks, e-government connectivity, smart city sensing and
emergency services networks. These are frequently subsidised, which introduces
state aid rules, open access obligations and reporting requirements that shape
technical design.

10.7 Submarine and International

Submarine cable is a distinct business with distinct dynamics: very large
up-front capital, 25-year design life, consortium or private ownership,
and, increasingly, hyperscaler rather than carrier funding. Content providers
now own or co-own a substantial share of new transoceanic capacity, having
moved from being customers of carriers to being builders.

For a national carrier, the relevant questions are landing station access,
backhaul from landing stations to major metros, route diversity across
different cable systems, and resilience against cable cuts - which are
disproportionately caused by fishing and anchoring in shallow water and have
become a geopolitical concern.

10.8 Emerging and Adjacent

Fibre sensing as a service: monetising the acoustic and strain sensing
capability of installed fibre for perimeter security, dig detection,
infrastructure health monitoring and seismology.

Private optical networks for industrial campuses, ports and airports,
frequently bundled with private 5G.

Quantum key distribution over fibre, currently a niche with real physical
constraints on distance and key rate, but of active interest to financial and
government customers and a driver of national testbed funding.

11. ECONOMICS AND COMMERCIAL MODELS
================================================================================

11.1 The Metrics That Govern Decisions

Homes passed. Premises where the network runs close enough that a connection
requires only a drop. The denominator for coverage obligations and the primary
unit of build progress.

Homes connected. Premises actually taking service. The denominator that
matters financially.

Take rate. Connected divided by passed. The single most sensitive variable in
any FTTH business case. A build modelled at 40 percent take rate that achieves
25 percent typically does not clear its cost of capital, regardless of how well
it was engineered.

Cost per home passed. Ranges very widely - roughly 250 to 600 currency units
per premise in dense urban areas with good duct availability, and two to four
times that in rural or duct-poor environments. Dominated by civil works.

Cost per home connected. Adds the drop, the ONT, installation labour and
activation. Typically another 150-400 per premise.

ARPU, churn and customer lifetime value. The revenue side, against which the
above is amortised over 10-20 years.

11.2 The Structure of the Case

The characteristic difficulty of fibre investment is that cost is front-loaded
and certain, while revenue is back-loaded and uncertain. Payback periods of
7-12 years are normal, which places fibre in a different investment category
from most telecom spending and explains why it attracts infrastructure funds
rather than growth investors, why public subsidy is common in rural areas, and
why operators sequence builds by expected take rate rather than by engineering
convenience.

Levers that improve the case:

  - Pre-marketing and demand aggregation before building a street, so that
    take rate is known rather than assumed.
  - Wholesale revenue on the same asset, raising utilisation per home passed.
  - Reuse of existing duct, pole and building infrastructure.
  - Phased split-ratio and OLT population, deferring active capex until
    subscribers connect.
  - Subsidy and public funding in areas that cannot be built commercially.
  - Copper decommissioning, which converts a parallel operating cost into a
    saving and frees exchange real estate - often the largest single opex
    benefit, and frequently delayed by regulatory obligations to maintain
    legacy service.

11.3 Operating Cost Structure

Relative to copper access, fibre reduces energy consumption per subscriber
substantially (a passive splitter consumes nothing; a field-powered DSLAM
cabinet consumes continuously and needs batteries), reduces fault rate per
subscriber per year by roughly an order of magnitude, and removes the line
plant maintenance activities that dominate copper opex.

It introduces new costs: ODN record-keeping, specialised splicing skills, and
the operational complexity of managing multiple PON generations coexisting on
one plant during migration.

11.4 Migration Economics

Most incumbents are not building greenfield; they are migrating. The decision
sequence is typically:

  1. Overlay XGS-PON on existing GPON plant using coexistence elements,
     avoiding any outside plant change.
  2. Migrate high-value and high-usage subscribers first, which relieves
     congestion on the GPON port and frees capacity for the remainder.
  3. Move business and mobile transport onto the same infrastructure to raise
     utilisation.
  4. Introduce 50G PON where capacity or competitive pressure justifies it,
     initially on selected ports rather than network-wide.
  5. Decommission GPON on a port-by-port basis once the residual population is
     small enough to migrate in a single campaign.

The important principle is that the ODN is not touched at any step. That is the
whole architectural point of wavelength coexistence, and it is why ODN design
quality determines how cheaply an operator can execute the next twenty years of
technology transitions.

12. STANDARDS BODIES AND THE ECOSYSTEM
================================================================================

12.1 Who Specifies What

ITU-T Study Group 15. Optical transport and access. Owns the G-series: fibre
specifications (G.65x), WDM grids (G.694), OTN (G.709), PON (G.984, G.987,
G.9807, G.989, G.9804), synchronisation (G.826x, G.827x) and management models.
The authoritative source for carrier optical networking.

IEEE 802.3. Ethernet, including EPON (802.3ah, 802.3av, 802.3ca) and the
Ethernet PHY specifications that define grey optics. IEEE 1588 defines
precision time protocol.

Broadband Forum. Access architecture and management: TR-101/TR-178
architecture, TR-069/TR-369 device management, conformance and interoperability
testing for PON, and the cloud CO and disaggregation work.

OIF. Implementation agreements for coherent interfaces - 400ZR, 800ZR/ZR+,
1600ZR - and for the electrical and optical interfaces inside modules. The body
that made coherent pluggables interoperable rather than vendor-specific.

FSAN. An operator and vendor forum that develops PON requirements which are
then standardised in ITU-T. Effectively the requirements front end of the PON
standards process.

MEF. Carrier Ethernet service definitions and, more recently, SD-WAN and
lifecycle service orchestration frameworks.

Telecom Infra Project. Operator-driven open designs including disaggregated
transport (Phoenix, Cassini/Galileo family), open optical line systems, the
GNPy physical layer modelling library, and open access architectures.

Open Networking Foundation and successor communities. SDN and disaggregation,
including VOLTHA, SEBA and TAPI.

OpenROADM MSA. Operator-led interoperability specifications and YANG models for
multi-vendor ROADM networks.

IETF. Transport-relevant protocol and model work: NETCONF, YANG, ACTN, PCE,
segment routing.

IEC and TIA. Component, connector, cable and test method standards, including
the connector cleanliness grades used in field practice.

12.2 The Supply Chain

Fibre and cable: Corning, Prysmian, Sterlite Technologies, Furukawa/OFS,
Fujikura, Sumitomo, YOFC, Hengtong.

Systems - transport and access: Nokia, Ericsson, Huawei, ZTE, Ciena, Cisco,
Adtran, Calix, Infinera, Fiberhome, Juniper, Nokia's and others' disaggregated
offerings.

Optical components and modules: Coherent, Lumentum, Broadcom, Marvell,
Semtech, Source Photonics, Hisense, Accelink, Eoptolink, Innolight.

DSP and silicon: Marvell, Broadcom, Cisco/Acacia, Nokia, Huawei/HiSilicon,
plus merchant PON silicon from several suppliers.

Test and measurement: EXFO, VIAVI, Anritsu, Yokogawa, Fluke Networks.

Two structural observations. First, geopolitics has fragmented this supply
chain: restrictions on Chinese vendors in several markets have reshaped
procurement, raised costs and slowed some deployments, while accelerating
domestic manufacturing initiatives elsewhere. Second, the component layer has
consolidated significantly, so that a small number of DSP and optical component
suppliers underpin most systems vendors' roadmaps - a concentration risk that
carriers increasingly consider explicitly in vendor selection.

13. TECHNOLOGY DIRECTIONS TO 2030
================================================================================

13.1 Access

50G PON moves from early deployment to volume. The initial asymmetric variant
(50G down, 25G up) addresses most near-term needs; symmetric 50G follows for
business and transport applications. The operational questions are wavelength
coexistence with the installed GPON and XGS-PON base, optics cost curves, and
whether the market will support a further generation beyond 50G in the TDM-PON
line or will move to coherent access.

Coherent PON is under active research and early standardisation discussion. It
would bring digital coherent techniques - and therefore very large power
budgets and much higher split ratios or reach - into the access network. The
obstacle is cost and power per ONU, which must fall by orders of magnitude
relative to transport-grade coherent to be viable at subscriber scale. The
likely first applications are aggregation and business rather than mass-market
residential.

Fibre deeper into the mobile network, with PON-based fronthaul maturing and
converged access nodes serving residential, business and cell sites from one
platform, is a continuing direction rather than a discrete event.

13.2 Transport

800G per wavelength has become the mainstream high-capacity channel, and 1.6T
is entering the deployment window, with shipment volumes and chipset revenues
now rising sharply. Baud rates above 200 Gbaud, higher-order constellations,
probabilistic constellation shaping and improved soft-decision FEC continue to
push spectral efficiency towards practical limits.

Spectrum extension is the other axis. C+L is standard on high-capacity routes;
S band extension is under development. Since spectral efficiency is approaching
its information-theoretic ceiling, capacity growth increasingly comes from more
spectrum and more fibre rather than from more bits per symbol.

Space-division multiplexing - multicore fibre with several cores in one
cladding, and few-mode fibre using multiple spatial modes - is the longer-term
answer to fibre exhaust. Multicore is already used in submarine trials and in
some data centre research deployments; the constraints are splicing, connector
and amplifier technology for multicore, which are maturing but not yet routine
field practice.

Hollow-core fibre has moved from laboratory curiosity to industrial
scale-up. Guiding light in air rather than glass gives roughly a third lower
propagation delay, very low nonlinearity, high power handling and, in the most
recent nested antiresonant nodeless designs, attenuation below that of solid
silica - published figures have gone under 0.1 dB/km, with even lower losses
reported in research. Microsoft has been the most visible driver, having
acquired Lumenisity in 2022 and subsequently scaled HCF manufacturing with
partners to supply its own network; euNetworks, Comcast and BT have run
operational routes and trials. The near-term applications are latency-sensitive
financial routes, data centre interconnect and AI cluster interconnect. Broad
carrier access use is further out and depends on cable, splicing and connector
ecosystems, field skills and standards catching up.

13.3 Architecture and Operations

Pluggable coherent continues to displace transponders, pushing optical function
into routers and compressing the traditional transport layer.

Optical switching moves into the data centre as AI cluster topologies seek
power and cost relief from electrical switching.

Autonomous network operation progresses along the TM Forum levels: from
assisted operation, through partial and conditional autonomy, towards
closed-loop systems that detect degradation, compute a remedy, evaluate it
against a digital twin and implement it without human intervention. Most
carriers are realistically operating between conditional and high autonomy for
specific, well-bounded loops - optical restoration, power optimisation,
capacity augmentation triggers - rather than across the network as a whole.

Energy efficiency has become a first-order design constraint rather than a
reporting exercise, driven by electricity cost, sustainability commitments and,
in some markets, the physical unavailability of additional grid power at
existing sites. Metrics have shifted from watts per port to watts per gigabit
per kilometre, and equipment selection now weighs power alongside capacity.

Security concerns extend from the logical to the physical: fibre tapping
detection, cable route resilience, submarine cable protection, and the
long-horizon question of post-quantum cryptography for the control plane and
for the encryption applied over optical links.

A.6 Design Rules of Thumb

    - Budget 3 dB of repair margin on any access plant; defend it.
    - Split ratio, not distance, usually limits urban PON design.
    - Civil works dominate FTTH capex; optimise digging before optics.
    - Never mate APC to UPC.
    - Inspect every connector end face before every mating.
    - Bend faults appear first at the longest wavelength - test at 1625 nm.
    - Measure splice loss bidirectionally and average.
    - Design the ODN for thirty years; the electronics will change three times.
    - A digital twin built on inaccurate inventory is worse than no twin.
    - Take rate, not homes passed, determines whether the build was worth it.
